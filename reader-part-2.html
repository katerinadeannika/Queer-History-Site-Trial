1920–1929

The City Speaks Back

Weimar Berlin, Paris, Harlem, Tokyo, Shanghai, and Los Angeles became dense but unequal worlds of magazines, cabarets, bars, homes, theaters, salons, and studios. The decade joins public nightlife with domestic and creative partnerships, including Cather and Lewis, Barnes and Wood, Haines and Shields, and a broad network around de Acosta.

“Visibility was not equality.”

Key figures and works: Hirschfeld and Giese, Lotte Hahm, Woolf and Sackville-West, Cather and Lewis, Barnes and Wood, Dietrich, Haines and Shields, Rainey, Smith, Bentley, Orlando, and The Well of Loneliness.

1930–1939

The Archive Burns

Authoritarianism gathered older laws, medical fears, racism, antisemitism, and police power into increasingly violent systems of repression. The destruction of Hirschfeld’s Institute became a symbol of loss, while exile, fiction, film, domestic partnership, and memory preserved other traces.

“Archives are never only shelves. They survive in exile, in novels, in photographs, and in memory.”

Key figures and works: Hirschfeld and Giese, Lorca, Barnes, Bentley, du Maurier, Dietrich, de Acosta, Haines and Shields, Nightwood, Mädchen in Uniform, Rebecca, and Morocco.

  • USHMM: 1935 Paragraph 175 revision

1940–1949

War Changes the Map

Total war displaced people, intensified persecution, and reshaped queer geography through ships, barracks, factories, hospitals, ports, ruins, and postwar cities. Liberation from Nazism did not automatically end criminalization for survivors.

“The end of the war ended one regime of terror. It did not end the laws, files, fears, and institutions.”

Key figures and works: pink-triangle survivors, Tennessee Williams, Harry Hay, Kewpie, Daphne du Maurier, Gertrude Lawrence, Carson McCullers, Annemarie Schwarzenbach, and Marlene Dietrich.

1950–1959

The Room Where People Met

In the Cold War, a living room, bar, post-office box, magazine, or borrowed apartment could be both refuge and danger. The Lavender Scare, Wolfenden inquiry, bar raids, licensing rules, and homophile organizing shaped how people built recognition and survival.

“A room could be a refuge and a trap.”

Key figures and works: Hay, Mattachine, Martin and Lyon, Daughters of Bilitis, Baldwin and Delaney, Christine Jorgensen, ONE Magazine, The Ladder, and lesbian pulp.

  • National Archives: Lavender Scare
  • ONE Archives
  • GLBT Historical Society
1960–1969

The Door Opens

Partial reform did not end policing, employment discrimination, or forced displacement. The decade includes homophile pickets, Compton’s Cafeteria, the Black Cat Tavern, Stonewall, Rustin, Baldwin, lesbian organizing, Tokyo, Francoist Spain, and District Six.

“By 1969, people were no longer only trying to survive the law. Increasingly, they were confronting it.”

Key figures and works: Frank Kameny, Bayard Rustin, James Baldwin, Martin and Lyon, Marsha P. Johnson, Sylvia Rivera, Stormé DeLarverie, Compton’s Cafeteria, and Stonewall.

Across eras

Threads Across Eras

The comparative history does not move in a straight line from silence to freedom. Across Europe, North America, Asia and the Pacific, the Middle East and North Africa, Africa south of the Sahara, and Latin America, visibility and vulnerability repeatedly coexist.

“Their history is not only what authorities recorded. It is what people made possible.”
Official records often show the state’s attempt to control people. Community photographs, letters, periodicals, oral histories, music, performance, and local archives can show worlds people made despite that control.
