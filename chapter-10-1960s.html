Chapter 10 — 1960–1969: The Door Opens

Cold open

Picture: A police raid; protest placards; the Stonewall Inn; British parliamentary reform; a District Six removal notice; a Tenderloin cafeteria counter; a March on Washington planning table.

Narration: “By 1969, people were no longer only trying to survive the law. Increasingly, they were confronting it.”

Narration, continued: “The door did not open everywhere, and it did not open for everyone. But across the decade, people challenged police power, made new organizations, entered public debate, and refused the idea that a private life should be grounds for punishment.”

Act I — Laws and reform

Cover the United Kingdom’s Sexual Offences Act 1967. The law partially decriminalized private consensual sexual acts between men over twenty-one in England and Wales, acting on the Wolfenden Report’s recommendation a decade later. Its limits must remain visible: it did not extend to Scotland or Northern Ireland; it excluded the armed forces and Merchant Navy; and its definition of “private” enabled continued surveillance and prosecution.[1]

Cover German reforms with precision. East Germany’s 1968 criminal code moved toward decriminalization; West Germany revised Paragraph 175 in 1969 so that consensual acts between men over twenty-one were no longer criminal offenses. These reforms did not erase past convictions, end stigma, or establish equality. Show the legal timeline beside portraits or testimonies of people whose lives had already been shaped by the older law.[2]

In the United States, sodomy laws and local vice enforcement remained in force. Police raids on bars, harassment of transgender and gender-nonconforming people, licensing pressure, and entrapment continued. Legal change and public protest grew together, but the decade did not produce nationwide protection.

Francoist Spain continued to police sexuality through public order, vagrancy, censorship, and surveillance, while the Ley de Peligrosidad y Rehabilitación Social would formalize a new repressive framework in 1970. Apartheid South Africa’s racial classification, housing control, pass laws, and forced removals intersected with sexual policing and determined whose neighborhood, household, and community could survive.

Suggested narration: “Reform was real. So were its limits. A law could remove one crime from the statute book and leave the police, the employer, the landlord, and the family with power over a life.”

Act II — City worlds: resistance has a geography

New York: Follow Greenwich Village and Harlem through bars, apartments, streets, theaters, and organizing spaces. Stonewall should appear as a 1969 uprising against a police raid and as a catalyst for new organizing—not as the first act of queer resistance or as the work of one person. The people present included patrons, bar workers, street queens, drag performers, butch lesbians, homeless youth, hustlers, and neighborhood residents; their individual actions cannot always be reconstructed with certainty.

San Francisco: Add the 1966 Compton’s Cafeteria uprising in the Tenderloin. Transgender women, drag performers, sex workers, and other queer patrons resisted police harassment at a 24-hour cafeteria used by people often excluded from wealthier or more respectable venues. The exact sequence of events is contested, but its place in 1966 and its importance as a pre-Stonewall instance of collective resistance are well established. Do not assign the event to a single “first” person or an invented dramatic detail.[3]

Los Angeles: Include the Black Cat Tavern protests, which followed a 1967 New Year’s raid and police violence. These actions demonstrate that anti-police organizing was developing in multiple cities before Stonewall. Pair the Black Cat with the earlier 1951 Stoumen decision to show that legal challenges, bar survival, and public protest were overlapping strategies.

London: Soho and Earl’s Court sustained bars, clubs, rooms, and networks amid the limits of the 1967 reform. The Gateways Club can be introduced as a prominent lesbian venue, but it should not stand for all London lesbian life. Use different class and racial locations where sources permit.

New Orleans, Tokyo, Madrid, Seville, and District Six: New Orleans’s French Quarter continued as a mixed nightlife and performance world; Tokyo’s Ni-chōme developed as a more recognizable bar district; Madrid and Seville maintained clandestine scenes under Francoist surveillance; and District Six remained a vibrant community even after its 1966 declaration as a white area began the process of forced removal. The map should show how state policy moved people, not only where queer venues appeared.

Suggested narration: “A raid happened at a place. A protest began on a street. A law changed in a capital. Queer history is never only national—it is built in neighborhoods, doorways, transit lines, and rooms the state tries to close.”

Act III — People and constellations

Frank Kameny and homophile picketers: Kameny and other activists brought a direct challenge to federal exclusion and public stigma. The Annual Reminder pickets in Philadelphia, held from 1965 through 1969, used formal dress and disciplined signage to argue that homosexual people were citizens entitled to employment and dignity. Explain the strategy rather than treating it as timid prehistory: respectability was a tactical response to the conditions organizers faced.

Del Martin and Phyllis Lyon: Their partnership and organizational work bridge 1950s homophile politics to the changes of the late 1960s. They were organizers, editors, speakers, and a documented long-term couple. Use them to show that women’s activism did not arrive suddenly after Stonewall; it had infrastructures, publications, and relationships built over many years.

Bayard Rustin: Rustin’s work places queer history inside the Black freedom struggle. As a strategist, pacifist, labor organizer, and deputy director of the 1963 March on Washington, he helped shape a movement even as his sexuality made him vulnerable to exclusion and attack. Do not present him as only a hidden gay figure. His political ideas and organizing skill are central to the story.[4]

James Baldwin: Another Country (1962) brought interracial desire, bisexuality, male intimacy, race, and urban conflict into a major American novel. Baldwin’s public work also made him a powerful interpreter of American violence and belonging. His relationship with Beauford Delaney was a documented friendship and mentorship, not a confirmed sexual partnership; maintain that distinction.

Stonewall-era figures: Marsha P. Johnson, Sylvia Rivera, Stormé DeLarverie, and many less-recorded people belong in the narrative of the late 1960s and early 1970s. Do not assign the Stonewall uprising to a single person, a single “first brick,” or a neat origin myth. Much widely circulated Johnson and Rivera imagery dates after 1969; label dates and avoid using later portraits to illustrate events they do not document.

Couples and constellations: Couples in this decade can be organizers, coworkers, housemates, friends, lovers, and political allies. Use clear evidence standards. A shared address, a joint organization, a photograph, or a coded work can establish different things; do not let any one of them automatically prove private sexual conduct.

Act IV — Media: a movement learns to speak publicly

Use homophile newspapers, underground papers, television reporting, documentary interviews, novels, plays, films, and protest signs. Contextualize period terms—“homophile,” “homosexual,” “gay liberation,” “lesbian,” and “transvestite”—rather than flattening them into current terminology. When period terms are derogatory or medicalized, identify them as such in the narration or captions.

Homophile and liberation media: ONE Magazine, The Ladder, newsletters, picket photographs, flyers, and local newspapers show how the decade’s communication networks expanded. ONE’s 1958 Supreme Court victory against postal censorship belongs as a bridge from the 1950s: the right to circulate a magazine became part of the right to form a public.

Television and documentary interviews: Use television coverage as a record of how broadcasters framed homosexuality as problem, scandal, or emerging minority. Later interviews may provide crucial testimony about 1960s events, but they should be identified as retrospective accounts rather than treated as contemporaneous footage.

Literature and theater: Baldwin’s Another Country (1962), Jean Genet’s The Balcony (1956) and The Screens (1961), and Gore Vidal’s Myra Breckinridge (1968) show divergent ways fiction and theater unsettled sexual and gender norms. Explain the genre and context of each work; do not imply that all were received as “queer media” in one uniform way.

Protest imagery: Placards, chants, street photographs, and police lines can convey political transformation, but avoid editing them into a false climax in which Stonewall resolves the decade. The final movement should point forward: liberation groups, lesbian-feminist organizing, transgender activism, and backlash will all intensify in the 1970s.

Suggested narration: “By the end of the decade, the language had changed. People still faced raids and arrests. But more and more, they named those acts not as private misfortune, but as political injustice.”

Ending bridge

Move from a Stonewall street scene to the 1969 Annual Reminder picket, Compton’s Cafeteria, a District Six removal notice, a copy of The Ladder, and a city map with routes extending beyond the United States.

Narration: “The door opened because people pushed it—in bars, on sidewalks, in courtrooms, on stages, and in meetings that began behind closed doors. The next decade would make that struggle louder, wider, and more divided. But by 1969, silence was no longer the only way to survive.”

Research and ethics note

Do not reduce Stonewall to a single hero, a single brick, or a single night detached from earlier organizing. Do not use later portraits to claim contemporaneous visual evidence. Where accounts conflict, state that they conflict; where a name is missing, do not manufacture one.
