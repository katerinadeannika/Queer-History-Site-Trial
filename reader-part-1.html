Introduction

Why 1880?

1880 is a narrative and archival starting point, not an origin story. Industrial cities, mass print, modern criminal statutes, policing, sexology, empire, and photography increasingly converged. Those forces made queer networks more possible—and participants more vulnerable to classification, surveillance, and notoriety.

Archive principle: a police record may document an arrest, not self-understanding; a photograph may document a public image, not private conduct. The project distinguishes documented partnership, strongly supported intimacy, documented attachment or friendship, and rumor.

1880–1889

Before the Name

Late-Victorian law and public-order policing created uneven risks in Britain, Germany, France, and North America. London’s theaters, parks, hotels, railway stations, and restaurants made contact possible; Paris, Berlin, and Capri reveal different systems of class, travel, and discretion.

“The law did not create desire. It created new risks around it.”

Key figures and works: Oscar Wilde, John Addington Symonds, H. Rider Haggard, King Solomon’s Mines, and The Happy Prince and Other Tales.

1890–1899

The Trial That Named a World

Wilde’s trials converted a private network into international spectacle. The project distinguishes allegation, testimony, press rumor, and conviction while locating the case within wider city worlds in London, Paris, Berlin, New York, and New Orleans.

“The trial did not discover a secret world. It forced one into public view.”

Key figures and works: Wilde, Lord Alfred Douglas, Robbie Ross, Constance Wilde, Havelock Ellis, The Picture of Dorian Gray, The Importance of Being Earnest, and Sexual Inversion.

1900–1909

The Scientist, the Star, and the Suit

Hirschfeld’s reform work and sexology offered language and legal argument while also producing new classifications. In Paris, Colette and Mathilde de Morny—Missy—made gender performance, theatrical collaboration, and public scandal impossible to ignore.

“Recognition and regulation arrived together.”

Key figures and works: Magnus Hirschfeld, Colette, Missy, Renée Vivien, Natalie Barney, and Claudine à l’école. The Institute for Sexual Science belongs to 1919 onward; it should not be projected backward into this decade.

1910–1919

War Makes New Rooms

War disciplined bodies, separated people, and made secrecy dangerous. It also moved soldiers, nurses, workers, and migrants through barracks, hospitals, factories, leave routes, ports, and cities. The script treats military files as records of institutional power, not transparent identity documents.

“War made intimacy visible in letters, poems, and absence. It also made silence necessary.”

Key figures and works: Siegfried Sassoon, Wilfred Owen, Colette, Federico García Lorca, and the early Takarazuka Revue. Sassoon and Owen were documented friends and mentor/mentee; the evidence does not establish a romantic relationship.
