Chapter 8 — 1940–1949: War Changes the Map

Cold open

Picture: A pink triangle; troop ship; women dancing at a service club; bombed city blocks; an unopened letter; a demobilization queue; a District Six street photograph.

Narration: “War displaced entire populations. Some people found a language for themselves in that movement. Many more were silenced by it.”

Narration, continued: “For people marked by the Nazis as homosexual, liberation did not always mean freedom. For soldiers, nurses, factory workers, and civilians, wartime movement could open a door to intimacy—and close it again with a transfer order, a discharge, a border, or a letter that never arrived.”

Act I — Laws and total war

Nazi persecution intensified through 1945. Paragraph 175, strengthened in 1935, enabled the arrest and imprisonment of thousands of men accused of homosexuality; an estimated 5,000 to 15,000 were imprisoned in concentration camps, where researchers estimate that roughly two-thirds perished. Numbers must be presented as estimates and situated within the broader persecution of people classified as homosexual, rather than used as an isolated statistic of suffering.[1]

State plainly that liberation from fascism did not immediately liberate homosexual survivors. Paragraph 175 remained in force in both East and West Germany after the war. Men could continue to live with criminal records or face renewed prosecution, even after imprisonment under the Nazi state. East Germany repealed the provision in 1968; West Germany introduced limited reform in 1969; the statute was fully removed from unified German law only in 1994.[1]

Allied military systems also disciplined homosexual conduct. The exact rules and practices varied by service branch and country, but armed forces could investigate, court-martial, discharge, or otherwise penalize service members. Wartime necessity did not erase institutional prejudice; it often pushed it into uneven, local, and discretionary forms.

Postwar regimes frequently retained repressive statutes, police powers, and medical frameworks. In Spain, Francoist National-Catholic morality framed nonconforming sexuality as a threat to social order. Across colonial and semi-colonial settings, public-order and “morality” laws were used to demonstrate state discipline during a period of war, decolonization, and national reconstruction.

Suggested narration: “The end of the war ended one regime of terror. It did not end the laws, files, fears, and institutions that had made queer people vulnerable.”

Act II — Queer geographies of war and aftermath

Barracks, ships, hospitals, factories, service clubs, ports, black markets, occupied cities, and displaced-persons camps created spaces of encounter and danger. Do not present them as hidden utopias. They were structured by rank, race, gender, nationality, violence, and the constant possibility of transfer or accusation.

Wartime and postwar movement contributed to urban concentration. Some people who encountered queer social worlds near bases, ports, factories, or hospitals chose to remain in or relocate to cities after demobilization. Track movement toward London, New York, Los Angeles, Paris, Berlin, Tokyo, and Cape Town, while making clear that not everyone had the resources or legal ability to choose where to live.

In Berlin, ruins, occupation zones, and black markets created cover as well as danger. In Tokyo and Shanghai, defeat, occupation, reconstruction, and changing political regimes remade commercial nightlife and private social networks. Use cities as sites of unstable adaptation, not uncomplicated rebirth.

In Cape Town, District Six remained a multiracial working-class neighborhood in which queer social worlds would become more visible in later photographic and oral-history records. Kewpie, born in District Six in 1941, was a child during this decade. Do not show later drag photographs as if they document Kewpie’s 1940s life; use them only to introduce the archive and its later testimony to a community that was already taking shape.[2]

Suggested narration: “A map of war usually follows armies. This map follows letters, transfers, ships, rented rooms, and people who discovered that the city held others like them.”

Act III — People and couples

Pink-triangle survivors: Center survivors as individuals rather than symbols. Use testimony where available, with names, dates, and permissions. Explain that the pink triangle was a Nazi camp classification imposed on some prisoners; it was later reclaimed by activists, but its original meaning was persecution and state violence. Do not use it as generic visual decoration.

Service members’ letters: Letters can document affection, separation, shared routines, and wartime fear. They may not reveal a writer’s sexual identity or the full character of a relationship. Use quotations only where the repository, date, recipient, rights, and context are established; avoid turning unambiguous friendship into romance.

Tennessee Williams: Williams emerged in the 1940s as a major playwright. The Glass Menagerie (1944) and A Streetcar Named Desire (1947) brought longing, repression, masculinity, femininity, class, and vulnerability to a broad audience. His work can register queer emotional worlds without requiring the script to treat every character as a direct portrait of his private life.

Harry Hay: Hay’s political ideas belong at the end of the decade. In August 1948 he drafted a prospectus imagining an organization for homosexual people; the Mattachine Society emerged from the work of Hay and others beginning in 1950. The script should present 1948 as a moment of conception and organizing thought, not incorrectly state that Mattachine was already founded during the war.[3]

Kewpie: Kewpie’s childhood is a reminder that the documentary’s chronology is not only a sequence of famous adult lives. The GALA Queer Archive holds Kewpie’s personal photographic collection, built principally from the 1950s through the early 1980s, which documents a visible and integrated District Six queer community. The 1940s episode may introduce Kewpie’s birth and place, then reserve the photographic archive’s full visual story for later decades.[2]

Daphne du Maurier and Ellen Doubleday: In the late 1940s, du Maurier developed an intense attachment to Ellen Doubleday, wife of her U.S. publisher Nelson Doubleday. The biographical record documents du Maurier’s desire more securely than it documents reciprocity or a sexual relationship. Present this as documented attraction or unreciprocated love, not as a confirmed affair.[4]

Daphne du Maurier and Gertrude Lawrence: Lawrence starred in du Maurier’s September Tide in 1948. Their relationship was emotionally intense. Du Maurier’s biographer Margaret Forster interpreted letters as suggesting physical intimacy, while the Daphne du Maurier Society cautions that the surviving evidence cannot conclusively establish it. This is a strong example of the script’s evidence standard: closeness and desire are documented; the full nature of the relationship remains contested.[4][5]

Carson McCullers and Annemarie Schwarzenbach: McCullers’s attachment to Schwarzenbach, the Swiss writer, photographer, traveler, and anti-fascist, is another case of powerful but asymmetrical feeling. The record supports McCullers’s deep attraction and Schwarzenbach’s importance to her imagination; it does not establish a completed, mutual affair. Use their story to show that queer history includes longing, friendship, and unfinished possibility as well as documented couples.[6]

Marlene Dietrich: Dietrich’s wartime work provides a political counterpoint to celebrity rumor. A German-born performer who became a U.S. citizen in 1939, she used her fame for anti-Nazi broadcasts, fundraising, and performances for Allied troops. Her wartime role should be centered as political action, while her queer-icon status and private relationships remain contextual rather than the whole story.[7]

Act IV — Media: intimacy, propaganda, and the postwar diagnosis

War films, propaganda, radio, letters, postwar memoirs, and psychiatric literature show both intense same-sex bonds and the postwar impulse to pathologize them. The script must not imply that homoerotic audience readings reveal a filmmaker’s intention or a character’s identity. Rather, explain how wartime narratives of male loyalty, female service, separation, and grief could become meaningful to queer viewers.

Featured media: letters and radio. A letter can be a material bridge across distance; a radio broadcast can carry propaganda, news, music, and private listening into a shared public crisis. Use period sound only after item-level clearance. If a broadcast contains hate speech or propaganda, contextualize it with a clear visual and narration identifying the speaker, purpose, and historical stakes.

Featured media: postwar psychiatry. Medical and sociological studies often treated homosexuality as a problem to diagnose, contain, or cure. Show covers, diagrams, and excerpts as historical evidence of professional authority—not as authoritative descriptions of queer people. Pair them with personal voices, where available, to prevent the episode from reproducing the medical gaze unchallenged.

Featured media: du Maurier’s theater. September Tide (1948) allows the episode to connect women’s creative collaboration, stage performance, and an archive of private feeling. Do not claim that a play proves an affair; frame it as the public work around which an intimate and contested relationship developed.

Featured media: Dietrich at war. Use troop-performance photographs, broadcasts, and original graphics to show how a performer’s persona could become anti-fascist cultural work. Her uniforms, trousers, songs, and presence with troops may resonate with her earlier androgynous image, but do not make costume a substitute for biography.

Suggested narration: “War made private feeling harder to keep private. After the war, institutions tried to name, diagnose, and control what people had discovered about themselves. But letters, performances, and memories carried other meanings forward.”

Ending bridge

Show an unopened letter placed beside a demobilization ticket; then cut to a Mattachine prospectus draft, a postwar city map, and a District Six street.

Narration: “The war changed the map. It scattered people, broke families, and destroyed communities. It also left behind routes—between cities, between letters, between people who had learned that they were not alone. In the next decade, some of those routes would become organizations, bars, and movements.”

  • Universal Newsreel search; World War II film search; Library of Congress World War II photographs: discovery sources only. Verify the exact item, creator, date, rights, restoration, and caption before use.

  • 1939 New York World’s Fair color home movie: marked as licensing material, not public domain. Treat it as a commercial-clearance lead.
  • District Six Museum and GALA Queer Archive: Kewpie Collection: contact directly for item-level access, permissions, captions, and community context. Do not crop or recaption Kewpie images without consultation.
  • Portrait leads: Tennessee Williams, Harry Hay, Marlene Dietrich, Daphne du Maurier, Carson McCullers, and Annemarie Schwarzenbach. Verify every individual file.
  • Music plan: original orchestration plus demonstrably reusable older recordings. Commercial 1940s swing, radio, and film music typically require licensing of the recording and composition separately.

Research and ethics note

Use composites sparingly. Label a composite in narration and on screen, root it in identifiable documentary patterns, and never place invented words in the mouth of a survivor. Do not make a pink triangle, bombed city, or private letter into visual shorthand for all queer wartime experience.
