Chapter 4 — 1900–1909: The Scientist, the Star, and the Suit

Cold open

Picture: Magnus Hirschfeld at a desk; Colette and Mathilde de Morny (“Missy”) in theatrical costume; a Paris theater program; a silent-film city street; medical diagrams and a newspaper caricature placed side by side.

Narration: “At the start of the twentieth century, new language promised recognition. A doctor could call desire an inborn condition rather than a crime. A performer could step onstage in a suit and make gender into spectacle. But every new name came with a question: did it open a door—or build a new cage?”

On-screen text: Medical language could offer sympathy and recognition. It could also classify, pathologize, and control.

Act I — Laws and sexology: language as aid and constraint

Show how sexological ideas moved among expert books, popular newspapers, courtroom testimony, police files, and private correspondence. The central point is not that medicine “discovered” queer people. Rather, doctors, writers, activists, and police increasingly competed to describe desires and gender expressions that people had already lived in many forms.

Present Magnus Hirschfeld as a physician, sexologist, and reformer working against Paragraph 175. In 1897, he helped establish the Scientific-Humanitarian Committee, whose campaign sought repeal of the German law criminalizing sexual acts between men. By 1899, the committee was publishing the Yearbook for Intermediate Sexual Types, establishing a print forum for sexological research and reform advocacy.[1]

The episode should show the double edge of this work. Hirschfeld’s approach challenged the view that homosexuality was simply vice or criminality, and it argued for legal reform. Yet sexology organized people into typologies—such as “sexual intermediaries”—that could make lived identities appear as objects of expert classification. The narration should neither celebrate sexology as uncomplicated liberation nor condemn it as only oppression.

Keep the chronology exact. Hirschfeld’s Institute for Sexual Science opened in Berlin in 1919, after the period covered by this episode. Karl Giese’s work and relationship with Hirschfeld also belong primarily to the 1920s, when Giese became involved in the Institute’s world. This chapter can foreshadow the later institution but must not show it as already existing in 1900–1909.[2]

Suggested narration: “The new science gave some people words for themselves. It gave the state new files, too. Recognition and regulation arrived together.”

On-screen documents: - A 1900s edition or period advertisement for Hirschfeld’s sexological work - Scientific-Humanitarian Committee petition or publication - Yearbook for Intermediate Sexual Types cover or title page - A newspaper caricature or hostile article, labeled as hostile reception - A legal timeline showing Paragraph 175 beside the Committee’s repeal campaign

Act II — City worlds: performance, reform, and urban anonymity

Berlin is the focal city for early reform politics and social networks. Show the city’s expanding transit, publishing, medical, and cultural infrastructure: lecture halls, publishers, cafés, apartments, theaters, and streets. Do not project the later Weimar-era bar culture backward into this decade. In 1900–1909, the story is one of emerging reform networks, print circulation, and urban possibility under a law that remained punitive.

Paris remains central for theater and gender performance. Café-concerts, music halls, and the Moulin Rouge made performance a public arena in which costume, voice, desire, and gender could be stylized. Visibility could attract audiences and press attention while also inviting moral regulation.

London shows post-Wilde caution. The 1895 trials did not eliminate queer life, but they intensified the perceived risks of blackmail, prosecution, and public exposure. Use streets, theaters, railway stations, restaurants, and private rooms to suggest a city of work and movement rather than a single hidden district.

New York’s Bowery and Greenwich Village begin a longer urban arc. Avoid treating Greenwich Village as already the mature bohemian or lesbian center of the 1910s and 1920s. Instead, introduce theater, lodging houses, migration, and commercial entertainment as conditions from which later worlds would grow.

Shanghai and Tokyo widen the geographic frame, but the map animation must not imply that sexological or European terms simply replaced local understandings. Show rail and shipping routes as channels for books, theater, commodities, and people, then state that ideas were translated, contested, and reshaped in local settings.

Act III — People and constellations

Colette and Missy are the episode’s central performance couple. Their relationship joined domestic intimacy, tailored clothing, artistic collaboration, celebrity, and public scandal. Missy—Mathilde de Morny, often known as “Max” or “Missy”—adopted masculine attire and performed gender nonconformity in ways that made clothing a public argument about who could be seen and how.[3]

In January 1907, Colette and Missy performed Rêve d’Égypte at the Moulin Rouge. A staged kiss during the pantomime provoked a scandal; accounts describe booing, disorder, and intervention by the police prefect, Louis Lépine, who threatened the venue with closure if the performance continued. The scene is useful because it demonstrates that theatrical gender play could be marketable and visible while a woman’s erotic gesture toward another woman remained vulnerable to official suppression.[3]

Do not isolate the kiss as a sensational spectacle. Begin with preparation: costumes, rehearsal, publicity, and the entertainment economy. Then show the consequences: press reaction, police pressure, and the relationship’s continued but changing public life. Colette and Missy remained partners until 1912, a duration that matters more than a single scandalous moment.[3]

Hirschfeld appears here as a different kind of public figure: a doctor and organizer whose work linked medicine, law reform, print culture, and private networks. Do not introduce Karl Giese as Hirschfeld’s partner in this episode; that relationship belongs to later chapters. This structure also makes the later Institute story more powerful: the viewer has already seen the ideas and networks from which it emerged.

Briefly introduce Renée Vivien and Natalie Clifford Barney as part of the wider Parisian literary constellation. Use precise chronology: their relationship began around the turn of the century, while Barney’s long-running rue Jacob salon became a more established institution in later decades. The scene should show a network of writers, performers, lovers, and patrons—not an anachronistic modern “community center.”

Suggested narration: “Colette and Missy made a public stage of what polite society wanted unseen. Hirschfeld tried to make an argument in print. One used performance; the other used science. Both discovered that visibility came with a cost.”

Act IV — Media: print, performance, and circulation

Use Claudine à l’école (1900), sexological books, postcards, cabaret reviews, theater programs, caricatures, and silent city footage. The media section should ask how images and texts moved across borders and social classes—and who had access to them.

Featured work: Colette, Claudine à l’école (1900). The novel’s schoolgirl world includes same-sex attraction and intimate female bonds. It was published under the name of Colette’s husband, Willy, and became the basis for a major commercial phenomenon. Do not describe it as a direct diary of Colette’s relationships; instead, explore how it made female desire and adolescent attachment visible in a popular literary marketplace.[4]

Featured work: sexological print. Use Hirschfeld’s essays, the Scientific-Humanitarian Committee’s publications, and Havelock Ellis’s Studies in the Psychology of Sex to show how scientific writing traveled. The production can animate a translation route, but the map should be captioned as a route of circulation, not proof that readers adopted a single identity or worldview.

Featured media: postcards and cabaret reviews. Postcards and publicity photographs could make gender ambiguity saleable. Reviews and police reports could turn the same ambiguity into scandal. Show the pair together to make the point: commercial imagery and moral panic were not opposites; they often fed one another.

Suggested narration: “A book could turn feeling into a case history. A postcard could turn it into a commodity. A stage performance could turn it into a scandal. The same city that sold an image could punish the person inside it.”

Research and rights note

Every image, performance still, postcard, magazine page, or film clip needs item-level provenance and clearance. Do not use medical diagrams, arrest records, or hostile caricatures as decorative “queer” imagery. Explain what the document was made to do, who made it, and what it cannot tell the viewer about a person’s private life.
